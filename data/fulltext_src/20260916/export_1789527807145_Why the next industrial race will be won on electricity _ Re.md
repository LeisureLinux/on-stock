# Why the next industrial race will be won on electricity | Reuters

> 来源：https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/why-next-industrial-race-will-be-won-electricity--ecmii-2026-09-15/

September 15 - For two centuries, industry has followed energy. Factories gathered around coalfields, refineries around ports and pipelines, energy-hungry industries went where fuel was plentiful and cheap. Google recently announced a 13 billion euros investment in digital and AI infrastructure in Finland, its largest single investment in Europe, alongside a 22-year nuclear power deal. The technology is new, but the industrial logic is old: reliable power attracts capital.

AI has made the return of electricity at the centre of economic policy impossible to miss. But data centres are part of a much wider surge. The International Energy Agency expects global electricity consumption to grow at least 2.5 times faster than overall energy demand through to 2030, propelled by industry, electric vehicles, cooling, data centres and other new uses. After decades of sluggish electricity growth in many advanced economies, power demand is rising again.

Ember’s new Age of Power report argues that the digital revolution and electrification of the physical economy are beginning to converge. Chips, batteries, power electronics, motors and sensors now underpin technologies ranging from AI servers to electric cars and grid batteries – expansion in one market can drive down costs and improve capability in others. Economies that can combine digital capability with cheap, scalable electricity and rapid electrification, Ember argues, will be better-placed to attract investment and build the industries that grow around them.

That will reshape the geography of economic power. Coal, oil and gas gave some countries an advantage by geological accident and left much of the world dependent on fuel shipped across oceans or through pipelines.

Sun and wind are spread far more widely: Ember calculates that 97% of people live in countries with renewable resources capable of producing more than 10 times their current electricity demand. Turning that physical potential into economic advantage depends on things countries can actually build and improve, such as generation, grids, storage, skills, markets and institutions.

In China , electricity already supplies 28% of its final energy consumption, compared with 22% in the United States and 21% in the European Union. Chinese industry is also becoming more electricity-intensive. The factories producing solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles consumed more than 300 terrawatt hours (TWh) in 2024 – about as much electricity as Italy uses in a year. It is also pursuing electrification and automation together, a combination that Ember argues can accelerate growth in both.

Europe has large renewable resources and some of the world’s most ambitious climate policy, but expensive power continues to bear down on industry. The European Central Bank estimates that electricity prices for energy-intensive industries rose by around 53% between 2019 and 2024. Their electricity consumption fell by about 14.5% between 2019 and 2023.

A continent trying simultaneously to decarbonise, re-industrialise and compete in new technologies cannot risk high electricity prices becoming a competitive disadvantage.

Business leaders already see electrification through this competitive lens. Recent global polling of business executives found 90% expect their operations to be largely electrified by 2035, 88% believe electrification will make their businesses more competitive and 91% expect it to deliver greater energy security.

Yet 72% say government policy is moving too slowly, and 62% would consider moving operations to another country if they could not get enough support to electrify.

The pace of electrification will be front and centre at Climate Week NYC. Energy and electrification are set to run through the week’s business and political agenda, while the incoming COP31 presidency in Turkey has already made electrification one of its headline priorities, setting a goal for electricity to supply 35% of global final energy demand by 2035.

Two obstacles to rapid progress stand out. The first is grids, with more than 2,500 gigawatts (GW ) of renewable generation, storage and large electricity loads currently sitting in connection queues worldwide. Major grid infrastructure can take five to 15 years to build.

The second is finance: in emerging and developing economies, the cost of capital for renewable power and batteries can be at least twice that in advanced economies. Cheap renewable resources are little advantage if businesses cannot connect to the power they produce.

For that reason, the next stage asks more of governments than adding gigawatts to renewable-energy targets. Power has to be affordable. New factories and equipment have to connect quickly. Networks have to use digital technology and flexible demand to get more from the wires already in the ground. Capital has to be cheap enough for countries with enormous renewable potential to turn it into productive electricity.

The economic map drawn by coal, oil and gas has lasted for generations; now electricity is beginning to redraw it. Natural resources will still matter, but institutions, infrastructure and investment will matter more. Countries that can make clean electricity cheaply, and move it where it is needed, will create an advantage that compounds as more of the economy goes electric.
